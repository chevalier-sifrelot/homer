**Mount Olympus** is the everlasting home of the gods, the lofty seat from which Jove and the other immortals watch over and intervene in the affairs of mortals.

## Appearances

Olympus is referenced throughout both the Iliad and the Odyssey. In the Iliad it appears from Book I onward as the gods' dwelling and council place (e.g. Books I and VIII). In the Odyssey it is described most fully in Book VI, and referenced in Books XI and XXIV.

## Significance

- It is portrayed as a serene, weatherless paradise, the eternal dwelling of the immortals: "Minerva went away to Olympus, which they say is the everlasting home of the gods. Here no wind beats roughly, and neither rain nor snow can fall; but it abides in everlasting sunshine and in a great peacefulness of light" (Odyssey, Book VI).
- Its stability is tied to the power of Jove, whose merest gesture shakes the whole mountain: "As he spoke the son of Saturn bowed his dark brows, and the ambrosial locks swayed on his immortal head, till vast Olympus reeled" (Iliad, Book I).
- It was the target of the giants Otus and Ephialtes, who sought to storm heaven itself: "They threatened to make war with the gods in Olympus, and tried to set Mount Ossa on the top of Mount Olympus, and Mount Pelion on the top of Ossa, that they might scale heaven itself" (Odyssey, Book XI).

## Inhabitants

- Jove (Zeus), who sits enthroned upon his golden throne and rules over gods and men from Olympus.
- Juno (Hera), queen of the gods, who dwells and takes counsel there.
- Minerva (Athena), who repeatedly departs from and returns to Olympus in the course of both poems.
